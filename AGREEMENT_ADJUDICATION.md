### Post sp_014

**Post:** "Times are down about 3% since the shoe rules changed, so obviously the shoes are the whole story and the sport has a problem."

**Staff label:** `analysis`  
**My label:** `hot_take`

**My call:** Staff is correct, but this is pile (c) - the rule is ambiguous here and my reading was defensible.

**Reasoning:**

This post sits exactly on the boundary that Decision Rule 1 addresses: "does the fact carry the argument?"

**Case for hot_take (my reading):**
- The word "obviously" signals sarcasm/dismissiveness rather than genuine analysis
- The conclusion ("shoes are the whole story") is presented as self-evident rather than argued
- "the sport has a problem" is an evaluative claim with no support beyond the stat
- The 3% feels like decoration on a pre-existing opinion rather than the foundation of the argument
- Rule 1 example: "Super shoes have ruined racing, times are down 3% since 2017" → hot_take (stat as flourish)

**Case for analysis (staff's reading):**
- The post explicitly cites a specific, checkable fact (3%)
- It links that fact to a specific event (shoe rule change) 
- The causal connection (rule change → time decrease) is the core claim
- The sarcasm may just be the delivery style, not the substance
- Rule 1 requires only that a fact be present and "do work," not that the tone be neutral

**Why staff is right:**
On re-reading Rule 1, the critical question is whether the 3% statistic is "load-bearing" in the argument. Staff's interpretation is that even delivered sarcastically, the argument rests on the statistical claim (times down 3% since rule change). Without that fact, the post would have no substance. My interpretation focused too much on tone (sarcasm) rather than structure (does the fact carry weight?).

**Classification: Pile (c) - Ambiguous boundary, my reading was defensible**

The sarcastic delivery genuinely makes the intent unclear - is this a person analyzing data skeptically, or making a hot take with a stat attached? Rule 1's "does the fact carry the argument" doesn't fully resolve cases where tone and structure point different directions. This reveals a gap: the taxonomy should specify whether sarcasm affects categorization when the structure is analytical.

**Learning:** I applied the rule too strictly on tone rather than structure. The presence of a specific stat linked causally to an event likely qualifies as analysis regardless of delivery tone. However, the rule itself could be clearer on this boundary case.
